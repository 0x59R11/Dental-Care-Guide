## What could a dark spot mean?

Color alone cannot diagnose caries or show whether a lesion is active. A dark groove can have several explanations:

- **Stained fissure:** pigment lies in a natural groove, while enamel may remain intact and sound.
- **Early carious lesion:** minerals have been lost but a physical hole has not formed. A non-cavitated lesion may sometimes be arrested and remineralized.
- **Cavitated lesion:** the surface has broken down and tooth structure is missing. Saliva cannot regrow that missing shape.
- **Arrested or inactive lesion:** a lesion is no longer progressing. Discoloration can remain.

These are not four mutually exclusive stages. **Depth and cavitation describe damage; activity describes what the lesion is doing.** An inactive lesion may be non-cavitated or cavitated. Dark does not mean inactive, and pale does not prove active disease.

Clinicians consider location, surface texture and luster, plaque stagnation, changes over time, individual risks, and other findings, sometimes including X-rays. These are professional assessments, not instructions to scrape or probe a tooth at home. Neither a photograph nor the absence of pain can establish a diagnosis.

## Three possible courses

**Progression:** repeated acidic conditions can make mineral loss outweigh protection. A lesion may grow deeper, at a rate that varies with the tooth and its environment. Progression is possible, not inevitable.

**Stabilization:** little or no change may be detectable over a period. Minerals can still move in and out; this is not a perfectly static microscopic balance or proof of complete recovery.

**Arrest:** an active lesion can stop progressing when conditions improve. Plaque control, effective fluoride use, fewer fermentable carbohydrate exposures, and appropriate preventive care may help. Follow-up remains important because conditions can change again.

Stopping progression, returning minerals to an early lesion, and **restoring lost tooth structure** are different outcomes. A lesion does not have to disappear visually to become inactive. A cavity can retain plaque and be difficult to clean; management depends on depth, activity, access for cleaning, and professional assessment. Some lesions can be managed without a filling; others need restorative or more extensive treatment.

## How deeper damage can reach the pulp

One possible course is early enamel mineral loss, surface breakdown or cavitation, dentin involvement, and then inflammation or infection of the **pulp**, the living tissue inside the tooth. Dentin can also be affected beneath an apparently intact surface: this is not a rigid visible sequence. Further disease may cause an abscess or threaten the tooth. There is no fixed countdown, and advanced damage or infection cannot be repaired through home care alone.

## When to get help

Arrange a dental assessment for a new or changing mark, suspected cavity, or recurring symptoms; do not wait for pain. Early lesions are often painless, and pain does not reliably measure depth or activity. Keep preventive visits tailored to your needs. Persistent pain or swelling needs prompt dental advice; a suspected abscess needs urgent care. Swelling with difficulty breathing or swallowing requires emergency medical help.
