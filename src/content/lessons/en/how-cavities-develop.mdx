## A community on the tooth

**Dental plaque is a biofilm**: microorganisms attached to a tooth in a sticky matrix they help build. Bacteria naturally live in the mouth; the tooth provides a surface where communities can accumulate. Plaque is not simply food debris, and rinsing does not replace brushing.

Some bacteria use **fermentable carbohydrates**, such as sugars and some starches, for energy. Their metabolism produces acids. A lower **pH** means more acidic conditions; these can dissolve enamel minerals, a process called **demineralization**.

**Biofilm + fermentable carbohydrates → bacterial acids → lower pH → enamel mineral loss.**

This is a simplified model, not a prediction that every snack creates a cavity. Biofilm composition, exposure patterns, saliva, fluoride, and the tooth surface all affect the result. Plaque being present does not establish that a tooth has an active carious lesion.

## Do bacteria wait for the next meal?

Think of a community receiving food deliveries. New carbohydrates can increase acid production, but some residents also have stored supplies. Bacteria remain in the biofilm and metabolism can continue between meals; some can use carbohydrate reserves. They are not simply switched on by eating and switched off afterward. Activity and acid production vary rather than staying at one level.

Meanwhile, saliva clears residues, buffers acids, and supplies minerals. Fluoride reduces mineral loss and supports early mineral recovery. Several hours without eating do not prove that plaque is harmless or that acid production has stopped.

## A changing balance

When mineral loss repeatedly exceeds protection and recovery, a **carious lesion** develops: an area damaged by the caries process. Early enamel lesions can have an intact surface and no pain. Better conditions may stop progression and allow some mineral recovery. Continued loss can eventually break the surface and create a **cavity**, or physical hole; missing structure does not regrow through saliva.

Reducing sugar exposure helps, but does not replace plaque removal. Other fermentable carbohydrates and stored reserves can supply fuel, and plaque can also cause gum inflammation. Cleaning manages the biofilm, while fluoride, dietary habits, saliva protection, and appropriate dental care help shift the balance. The goal is a healthier environment, not a sterile mouth.
