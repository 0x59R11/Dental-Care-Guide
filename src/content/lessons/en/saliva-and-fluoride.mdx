Saliva does more than make swallowing comfortable. It helps clear food, dilutes acids, and contains substances that **buffer** them: they make the mouth less acidic. It also supplies minerals such as calcium and phosphate that help maintain tooth surfaces.

When conditions are acidic, enamel can lose minerals. When conditions improve, minerals can return to early weakened areas. This is called **remineralization**. Mineral loss and recovery are ongoing processes, not a simple timer that resets after every meal.

**Fluoride** helps protect teeth by reducing mineral loss and supporting remineralization. Fluoride toothpaste brings it directly to tooth surfaces. This is why brushing with fluoride toothpaste has a protective role beyond removing plaque.

Protection has limits. Remineralization can help an early lesion while the surface is still intact; it cannot fill an established hole or regrow a broken edge. Vitamins do not provide a shortcut for rebuilding substantial missing tooth structure.

Mineral recovery does not mean a lesion has regained every mineral or its original appearance. Arrest means progression has stopped; restoration replaces missing shape. A dentist assesses these separately. Cleaning and fluoride complement each other, so careful brushing without fluoride misses an additional protective effect.

When saliva flow is reduced, the mouth loses some of this protection. Persistent dry mouth can have several causes, including medicines and health conditions. Drink water for comfort and talk to a dentist or clinician about ongoing symptoms. Do not stop prescribed medicine on your own. Finding the cause matters more than simply masking the feeling.
