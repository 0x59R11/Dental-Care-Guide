A tooth looks solid and simple from the outside. Inside, it has several layers with different jobs. The part you usually see above the gum is the **crown**. The **roots** anchor the tooth in your jaw.

The crown is covered in **enamel**, a very hard, mineral-rich tissue. It helps the tooth withstand chewing and protects the layers beneath. But hard does not mean indestructible: acids can remove its minerals, and physical forces can crack or wear it.

Under the enamel is **dentin**. It makes up much of the tooth and is less resistant to acid than enamel. Dentin contains tiny channels that connect toward the center. When it becomes exposed, hot, cold, or sweet foods may trigger sensitivity.

At the center is the **pulp**, soft living tissue containing blood vessels and nerves. These extend through narrow canals in the roots. Deep decay or injury can irritate or infect the pulp.

Enamel has no living cells that can grow a new layer. Early mineral loss can sometimes be repaired through mineral exchange at the surface, but a broken piece or a cavity cannot simply grow back. Different kinds of damage need different care; you cannot judge the health of a tooth by its color alone.
