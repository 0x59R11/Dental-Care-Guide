## Amount and frequency

Sugar does not drill a hole in a tooth by itself. Plaque bacteria use it and produce acids that remove tooth minerals. A diet high in free sugars increases the risk of tooth decay.

**Amount and frequency both matter.** Reducing the total amount of sugary food and drink is useful. So is avoiding repeated exposures, such as continuously sipping a sweet drink or frequently eating sticky sweets. A different eating pattern does not erase the effect of a large amount of sugar.

## Acid does not always come from sugar

There is another route to acid damage: the food or drink may already be acidic. Soft drinks, some sports drinks, and fruit juices can expose teeth directly to acids. This process is called **dental erosion**. Unlike the acid involved in cavities, these acids do not need to be made by bacteria.

A drink can therefore be sugar-free and still contribute to erosion. A sugary acidic drink can create both kinds of exposure. Reflux and repeated vomiting can also bring stomach acid into contact with teeth.

A sweet acidic drink can therefore create two challenges at once: its own acids can cause erosion, while its sugar fuels bacterial acid production. Removing the sugar reduces one source of fuel but does not remove the drink’s acids.

## Everyday choices

Choose water often, limit prolonged sipping, and avoid holding or swishing acidic drinks around your mouth. There is no single eating schedule that everyone must follow. Focus on practical changes that reduce exposure while keeping meals varied and nourishing.
